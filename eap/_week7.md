# Week 7

## Week 7-1

Discussion on Organic Food

Some intellectuals, if not too many, would always like to show their
superiority over the general public by endlessly installing progressive
doctrines in a condescending manner, specifically, in the domain of diet.
They tout the still-emerging technologies like genetic modification with
religious enthusiasm, hold responsible for African famine organic food lovers
thousands of miles away in Europe, and would readily ridicule as an ignorant
fool anyone who dares to doubt.

Enough is enough.
These prigs who wrote that progressive stuff for the Saturday Evening Post
know nothing about real farming than they do about hypocrising.
From a moral high ground, it is far too easy to blame ordinary law-abiding
citizens, who are only too worried about food safety, so recently contaminated
by abused technological advancement, and have no choice but to pay the organic
certified premium price with their daily shrinking income during this
undergoing hyperinflation, while at the same time keeping a reasonable distance
from topics so embarrassing to progressivists, such as why they are so
credulous about not fully verified new agricultural technologies whereas remain
extremely skeptical about conventional farming styles practiced for centuries
by our ancestors, or why they are so incapable of explaining the 21st century’s
most prominent food crisis in North Korea with their productivity theory. (1/n)

It is a pity that our elders' words always go unnoticed down the drain, as ours
will when we age.
We don't even bother to spare any patience to understand what our parents talk
about when they talk about their old times.
For me, there are only pieces of murmurs from my parents, who were horrified by
the too artificial flavor of vegetables picked up from a greenhouse or the
staggering speed a chicken can grow nowadays, recalling their childhood
sensation when they gobbed cucumbers cultivated without chemical fertilizer or
caught carp in a river as clean as nature intended it to be.
"I've seen things you people wouldn't believe" -
this will surely be their opening statement if they ever watched the film Blade
Runner.

Those murmurs were never said aloud, but I think of them often.
I wonder if my old man ever sensed my impatience when he shared his petty
complaints at table, even though he never let on.
I did not fully understand the underlying lament behind the grumbles before I
learned a thing or two about the brave new world we live in, so distorted that
people can barely recognize, and the irreversible trend of industrialization.
It's just too bad for those of the last generation that all their old memories
"will be lost in time, like tears in rain". (2/n)

The popularity of organic food with an increasingly growing customer base is to
be explained not least by people's suppressed yearning for long-lost
near-natural taste.
Indeed, organic vegetables grown in a simulated natural environment are by no
means as flawless as the original ones in terms of flavor but are still better
than common kinds sold by the bushel.
When you ever witnessed how a tomato flower turned into a tempting red fruit on
some market shelf in less than 50 days, during which numerous unknown chemical
agents ripened it, you know precisely why some tomatoes could taste like
nothing.
To such motive, yet another must be added.
From the fears of half-carelessly over-sprayed pesticides or herbicides or,
even worse, secretly introduced non-reportable ingredients whose potential risk
is still largely unidentified, organic food releases the anxious public.
Granted, contamination of, for example, manure fertilizer due to the overuse of
antibiotics and hormones on livestock or poultry does exist during organic
farming, but it is a kind of hazard which is known and manageable, if not
negligible. (3/n)

Whether biological farming means the eventual agricultural utopia worth pursuit
is hard to say.
Treacherous merchants tend to exaggerate, to some degree, the healthy benefits
of organically cultivated ingredients, such as containing more nutrients.
The general public, however fond of the organic taste they may be, is naturally
skeptical of the plain-looking organic luxury placed on some not-so-prominent
shelf in a shopping mall.
But most probably, it will all depend upon this farming style itself:
whether or not its process is over-refined, whether or not it adheres as
closely as possible to the ecological standards, in short, whether or not it
could keep its ultimate promise to serve affordable, worry-free products, even
under constant pressure from the capital.
Only good faith is likely to preserve the trust so dear to the hearts of
housewives lining up in front of a food shelf. (4/n)
